## Section 2: Plain-language summary

**[H2]** What this test is, in plain words

The endometrium is the lining of the uterus, where an embryo implants. Sometimes this lining has a long-lasting, low-grade inflammation called chronic endometritis. It is marked by immune cells called plasma cells in the tissue, and it has been linked to a range of microorganisms [2][3].

Most people with chronic endometritis have no symptoms, or only mild ones, so it is easy to miss [3]. It can only be confirmed by looking at the tissue itself.

The Endometrial Infection Test looks at a small biopsy of the lining for these signs. If it finds them, your doctor can decide whether treatment is needed before your next embryo transfer.

The test does not tell you why a past cycle did not work, and it cannot promise a better result next time. It answers one specific question about the lining.

## Section 3: For clinicians (anchor `#clinicians`)

**[H2]** For clinicians

### Indications

**[H3]** When to consider the EIT

**Repeated implantation failure (RIF).** ESHRE's 2023 good practice recommendations on RIF state that "assessment for chronic endometritis (CE) can be considered", and that CE "can be diagnosed by hysteroscopy, haematoxylin and eosin staining, and CD138-labelling" [1]. ESHRE describes RIF as the situation where transfer of embryos considered viable has failed to produce a positive pregnancy test often enough, in a specific patient, to warrant further investigation [1].

**Recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL).** ESHRE's 2022 RPL guideline reports CE prevalence of 7% to 58% in women with RPL, depending on the detection method. It concludes that further research, including randomised trials, is needed before screening for endometritis can be recommended [2]. Testing in RPL is therefore a case-by-case clinical decision.

**Other situations.** Unexplained infertility, or hysteroscopic findings suggestive of CE (such as micropolyps or stromal oedema), at the clinician's discretion. {{CONFIRM_CLIENT_INDICATIONS}}

**Not a routine test.** We do not recommend the EIT as a routine screen for every IVF patient.

## Section 4: For patients (anchor `#patients`)

**[H2]** For patients

**[H3]** Is this test for me?
Only your doctor can say. It is most often considered after several embryo transfers have not led to a pregnancy. Some doctors also consider it after repeated miscarriages. If you have questions, take this page to your next appointment.

**[H3]** What happens during the biopsy?
Your doctor takes a very small sample of the uterine lining, usually with a thin, flexible tube passed through the cervix. It is done at the clinic and takes a few minutes. Many people feel cramping, similar to period pain, for a short time. Your doctor will tell you how to prepare and what to expect afterwards. {{CLINICAL_REVIEW}}

**[H3]** When will I get my results?
The lab reports to your doctor within 7 to 10 days of receiving the sample. Your doctor will explain the result to you.

**[H3]** What happens if the result is positive?
Your doctor will discuss whether you need treatment, and when to plan your next transfer. Decisions about treatment are made by your doctor.

**[H3]** Questions you could ask your doctor
- Why do you think this test is useful in my case?
- What will we do differently if the result is positive, or negative?
- When in my cycle should the biopsy be done?
- Are there other tests we should consider first?

## Section 5: What the result can and cannot tell you

**[H2]** What the result can and cannot tell you

**Two-column box (left "Can", right "Cannot")**

**The result can tell you:**
- Whether signs of chronic endometritis were found in the sample, using the method and threshold stated on the report.
- {{IF_PCR_OR_CULTURE: Which organisms, if any, were detected.}}
- Whether this is a factor your doctor may want to address before the next transfer.

**The result cannot tell you:**
- Whether chronic endometritis caused a past failed transfer or miscarriage.
- Whether treatment will lead to a pregnancy or live birth.
- Anything about embryo quality, endometrial receptivity timing, or other causes of infertility.
- A full picture of the whole lining. A biopsy samples a small area, so a negative result does not fully rule out CE.

**Note under box:** Diagnostic criteria for chronic endometritis are not yet standardised. Results from different labs, methods or thresholds may not be directly comparable [1][4].

## Section 6: Evidence box (anchor `#evidence`)

**[H2]** What the evidence says

**Box style:** Green left border (`#218A6A`), light background. Title: "Evidence status: guideline-supported, with open questions".

- **ESHRE RIF recommendations (2023):** "Assessment for chronic endometritis (CE) can be considered." "If CE is diagnosed, treatment with antibiotics can be considered." ESHRE notes that conclusions are limited by the lack of standardised diagnosis [1].
- **ESHRE RPL guideline (2022):** more research is needed before screening women with recurrent pregnancy loss for endometritis can be recommended. Immunohistochemistry for CD138 "seems to be" the gold standard for diagnosis [2].
- **Treatment and outcomes, older studies:** a 2018 meta-analysis of observational studies in women with RIF (5 studies, 796 patients) found higher live-birth rates in women whose CE resolved after treatment than in those whose CE persisted. The evidence was limited and non-randomised [5].
- **Treatment and outcomes, newer data:** a 2026 retrospective cohort of 1,401 women (3,041 transfer cycles) found no significant improvement in live birth after antibiotic treatment for CE diagnosed by CD138 [6].
- **Diagnostic thresholds:** a 2023 meta-analysis found no agreed plasma-cell threshold. Counts of 5 or more per 10 high-power fields were associated with higher miscarriage risk [4].
- **Microbiome testing:** the UK HFEA rates endometrial microbiome testing "grey", meaning there is not enough good evidence to rate it [7]. {{SHOW_ONLY_IF_METHOD_IS_PCR_OR_MICROBIOME}}

**Our position, in one line:** Of our tests, chronic endometritis testing has the clearest guideline support. It is still a test for selected patients, not for everyone.

## Section 7: FAQ

**[H2]** Frequently asked questions

**[H3]** What is chronic endometritis?
A persistent, low-grade inflammation of the uterine lining. It is identified by plasma cells in the tissue and has been linked to a range of microorganisms [2][3].

**[H3]** Does chronic endometritis cause symptoms?
Often not, or only mild ones. That is why a biopsy is needed to find it [3].

**[H3]** Who should have this test?
It is most often considered after repeated implantation failure, as ESHRE suggests [1]. Your doctor may also consider it in some cases of recurrent miscarriage. It is not meant for every IVF patient.

**[H3]** How is the sample taken?
Your doctor takes a small biopsy of the uterine lining at the clinic, at a specific time in your cycle.

**[H3]** Can I take the test while on antibiotics?
Please tell your doctor about any recent antibiotics. {{CONFIRM_ANTIBIOTIC_WASHOUT}}

**[H3]** How long do results take?
7 to 10 days from the day the sample reaches our lab. The report goes to your doctor.

**[H3]** If the result is positive, will treatment help me get pregnant?
No test or treatment can promise that. Some studies found better outcomes when chronic endometritis was treated and resolved, and a large recent study did not [5][6]. Your doctor will explain what the result means for you.

**[H3]** If the result is negative, does that mean my lining is fine?
It means signs of chronic endometritis were not found in the sample. It does not assess other aspects of the lining or other causes of infertility.

**[H3]** Is this the same as an ERA or receptivity test?
No. Receptivity tests look at the timing of the implantation window. The EIT looks for inflammation. They answer different questions. See [ERFA, The Soil Test](/diagnostics/erfa-endometrial-receptivity-test/).

**[H3]** How much does it cost?
We do not publish prices. Please ask the clinic that orders your test.
